## What do the primary pages say about held-out scenarios, satisfaction scoring, digital twins, browser/DevTools-driven validation, and human involvement that the earlier notes lack?

### Takeaway
StrongDM's pages add three things the earlier notes lacked: twins are built "from API contracts and observed edge cases" and then validated "against the live dependency until we stop finding behavioral differences"; the loop "runs until the holdout scenarios pass (and stay passing)" with "a sample of the output, fed back into the inputs"; and code is treated as "opaque weights whose correctness is inferred exclusively from externally observable behavior". OpenAI's page adds that the agent records before and after videos of a bug, merges its own PRs, escalates "only when judgment is required", and works behind "minimal blocking merge gates", while stating "Humans always remain in the loop" and that human QA capacity was the bottleneck. None of the pages gives a satisfaction threshold, a judge model, a scenario count, who authors scenarios, or a defect rate.

### Cited Findings

**StrongDM: held-out scenarios and the loop**

- The stop condition is stated on the Principles page: "The loop runs until the holdout scenarios pass (and stay passing)". — [StrongDM Principles](https://factory.strongdm.ai/principles)
- The Principles page gives a three-part model, "Seed → Validation harness → Feedback loop. Tokens are the fuel." Validation: "Your validation harness must be end-to-end, as close to the real environment as possible: customers, integrations, economics." Feedback: "A sample of the output, fed back into the inputs. This closed loop allows your system to self-correct and compound correctness." Seed: "Historically, you might have called this a PRD or a spec. Today, this could equally be a few sentences, a screenshot, or an existing codebase." — [StrongDM Principles](https://factory.strongdm.ai/principles)
- StrongDM gives two reasons tests were not enough: "Tests are too rigid - we were coding with agents, but we're also building with LLMs and agent loops as design primitives; evaluating success often required LLM-as-judge" and "Tests can be reward hacked - we needed validation that was less vulnerable to the model cheating". — [factory.strongdm.ai](https://factory.strongdm.ai/)
- The "Validation Constraint", in full: "Given zero hand-written code and zero traditional review, we required a system that could: Grow from cascades of natural-language specifications; Be validated automatically without semantic inspection of source. Code was treated analogously to an ML model snapshot: opaque weights whose correctness is inferred exclusively from externally observable behavior. Internal structure is treated as opaque." — [StrongDM Techniques](https://factory.strongdm.ai/techniques)
- On who writes scenarios, the only direct evidence on StrongDM's site is an image caption: "Synthetic scenario curation and shaping interface". The site does not say in prose whether humans or agents author scenarios. Willison describes execution, not authorship: "With their own, independent clones of those services—free from rate-limits or usage quotas—their army of simulated testers could go wild. Their scenario tests became scripts for agents to constantly execute against the new systems as they were being built." — [factory.strongdm.ai](https://factory.strongdm.ai/); [Simon Willison, 7 Feb 2026](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Feb/7/software-factory/)
- Willison places the idea in older practice: "StrongDM's answer was inspired by Scenario testing (Cem Kaner, 2003)." and "It imitates aggressive testing by an external QA team—an expensive but highly effective way of ensuring quality in traditional software." (The Kaner attribution is Willison's; StrongDM's page does not name Kaner.) — [Simon Willison, 7 Feb 2026](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Feb/7/software-factory/)
- Willison's framing of the core question: "how can you prove that software you are producing works if both the implementation and the tests are being written for you by coding agents?" and "Having the agents write tests only helps if they don't cheat and assert true." — [Simon Willison, 7 Feb 2026](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Feb/7/software-factory/)
- Stanford states the hiding more strongly than StrongDM does: "They kept these descriptions hidden from the agents, so the agents could not simply memorize the answers." **StrongDM: Digital Twin Universe**

- How twins are built and checked, from StrongDM's own DTU page: "The key insight is to replicate behavior at the boundary. We build test doubles from API contracts and observed edge cases, then validate them against the live dependency until we stop finding behavioral differences." — [StrongDM DTU technique](https://factory.strongdm.ai/techniques/dtu)
- The DTU page lists four benefits: "High-Volume Validation: Thousands of scenarios per hour without rate limits or API costs"; "Dangerous Failure Modes: Test edge cases that would be impossible against live services"; "No Abuse Detection: Avoid triggering security controls while stress-testing integrations"; "Determinism: Replayable, controlled test conditions for every scenario". The Techniques index adds: "Clone the externally observable behaviors of critical third-party dependencies. Validate at volumes and rates far exceeding production limits, with deterministic, replayable test conditions." — [StrongDM DTU technique](https://factory.strongdm.ai/techniques/dtu); [StrongDM Techniques](https://factory.strongdm.ai/techniques)
- The twins have user interfaces and are populated with simulated users. Willison's screenshot description of the Slack twin: a "Slack-like interface titled "DTU Slack"" with "automated introduction messages from users with Okta IDs (e.g. @okta-u-423438-00001 ...), all timestamped 2025-11-12Z"; his text: "showing a stream of simulated Okta users who are about to need access to different simulated systems." — [Simon Willison, 7 Feb 2026](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Feb/7/software-factory/)
- What was being built: "The software they were building helped manage user permissions across a suite of connected services. This in itself was notable—security software is the last thing you would expect to be built using unreviewed LLM code!" — [Simon Willison, 7 Feb 2026](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Feb/7/software-factory/)
- StrongDM's economic argument for twins: "Creating a high fidelity clone of a significant SaaS application was always possible, but never economically feasible. Generations of engineers may have wanted a full in-memory replica of their CRM to test against, but self-censored the proposal to build it." — [factory.strongdm.ai](https://factory.strongdm.ai/)

**StrongDM: other verification-relevant details**

- Non-interactive runs need complete intent. Shift Work: "Separate interactive work from fully specified work. When intent is complete (specs, tests, existing apps), an agent can run end-to-end without back-and-forth." Examples of complete intent: "RFC 9113 for HTTP/2, often accompanied by validation suites (h2spec, nghttp2)", "Spec + Test Suite = Complete Intent", and "An existing application constitutes an executable specification." — [StrongDM Shift Work](https://factory.strongdm.ai/techniques/shift-work)
- The factory's agent is Attractor: "A non-interactive coding agent structured as a graph of phases. Runs end-to-end when the work is fully specified." Willison notes the public repo "contains no code at all—just three markdown files describing the spec for the software in meticulous detail". — [StrongDM Products](https://factory.strongdm.ai/products); [Simon Willison, 7 Feb 2026](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Feb/7/software-factory/)
- StrongDM assigns different models to different factory roles and publishes the table. As of September 28th, 2026 it lists, among others, "QA Orchestration" (gpt-5.6-sol), "Security review" (opus-5.5, high), "Architectural Critique" (opus-5.5, max) and "Sprint Planning" as "consensus(opus-5.5, gpt-5.6-sol)", with the note "Consensus operator refers to an LLM merge of the points from independent plans." So QA orchestration and security review are named model roles, and at least planning uses two vendors' models together. — [StrongDM Weather Report](https://factory.strongdm.ai/weather-report)
- Willison's earlier description of the then-unnamed team (confirmed as StrongDM by his 7 Feb update): "Nobody reviews AI-produced code, ever. They don't even look at it." "The goal of the system is to prove that the system works. A huge amount of the coding agent work goes into testing and tooling and simulating related systems and running demos." "The role of the humans is to design that system - to find new patterns that can help the agents work more effectively and demonstrate that the software they are building is robust and effective." "Some of them had 20+ years of experience as software developers working on systems with high reliability requirements". — [Simon Willison, 28 Jan 2026](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Jan/28/the-five-levels/)
- Willison's summary of the human role after his visit: "software engineers move from building the code to building and then semi-monitoring the systems that build the code." He adds that the October 2025 demos predate "the Opus 4.5/GPT 5.2 releases that made agentic coding significantly more reliable a month after those demos." — [Simon Willison, 7 Feb 2026](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Feb/7/software-factory/)

**OpenAI: agent-driven validation and observability**

- Why the validation tooling was built: "As code throughput increased, our bottleneck became human QA capacity. Because the fixed constraint has been human time and attention, we've worked to add more capabilities to the agent by making things like the application UI, logs, and app metrics themselves directly legible to Codex." — [OpenAI, 11 Feb 2026](https://openai.com/index/harness-engineering/)
- The observability stack, from the diagram alt text: "An app sends logs, metrics, and traces to Vector, which fans out data to an observability stack containing Victoria Logs, Metrics, and Traces, each queried via LogQL, PromQL, or TraceQL APIs. Codex uses these signals to query, correlate, and reason, then implements fixes in the codebase, restarts the app, re-runs workloads, tests UI journeys, and repeats in a feedback loop." — [OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/harness-engineering/)
- The end-to-end autonomous loop, with video evidence: "Given a single prompt, the agent can now: Validate the current state of the codebase; Reproduce a reported bug; Record a video demonstrating the failure; Implement a fix; Validate the fix by driving the application; Record a second video demonstrating the resolution; Open a pull request; Respond to agent and human feedback; Detect and remediate build failures; Escalate to a human only when judgment is required; Merge the change". OpenAI qualifies it: "This behavior depends heavily on the specific structure and tooling of this repository and should not be assumed to generalize without similar investment—at least, not yet." — [OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/harness-engineering/)
- Agents merge their own work: "They pull review feedback, respond inline, push updates, and often squash and merge their own pull requests." — [OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/harness-engineering/)
- Merge gates are deliberately light: "The repository operates with minimal blocking merge gates. Pull requests are short-lived. Test flakes are often addressed with follow-up runs rather than blocking progress indefinitely. In a system where agent throughput far exceeds human attention, corrections are cheap, and waiting is expensive. This would be irresponsible in a low-throughput environment. Here, it's often the right tradeoff." — [OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/harness-engineering/)
- Agents write the tests, the evaluation harnesses and the review comments: the list of what agents produce includes "Product code and tests", "Evaluation harnesses", "Review comments and responses" and "Production dashboard definition files". There is no held-out check or separate-author rule anywhere on the page. — [OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/harness-engineering/)
- Quality is tracked in documents the agents maintain: "A quality document grades each product domain and architectural layer, tracking gaps over time." "Design documentation is catalogued and indexed, including verification status". "Dedicated linters and CI jobs validate that the knowledge base is up to date, cross-linked, and structured correctly. A recurring "doc-gardening" agent scans for stale or obsolete documentation that does not reflect the real code behavior and opens fix-up pull requests." — [OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/harness-engineering/)
- How human judgment enters: "Human taste is fed back into the system continuously. Review comments, refactoring pull requests, and user-facing bugs are captured as documentation updates or encoded directly into tooling. When documentation falls short, we promote the rule into code". The acceptance bar: "As long as the output is correct, maintainable, and legible to future agent runs, it meets the bar." — [OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/harness-engineering/)
- Project facts the earlier notes lacked: "The first commit to an empty repository landed in late August 2025." The scaffold "was generated by Codex CLI using GPT‑5". "The product has internal daily users and external alpha testers. It ships, deploys, breaks, and gets fixed." "the product has been used by hundreds of users internally". "We estimate that we built this in about 1/10th the time it would have taken to write the code by hand." Another agent, "Aardvark", also works on the codebase. — [OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/harness-engineering/)
- OpenAI's stated open questions: "What we don't yet know is how architectural coherence evolves over years in a fully agent-generated system. We're still learning where human judgment adds the most leverage and how to encode that judgment so it compounds." — [OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/harness-engineering/)

- Shapiro's level 4 verification step is thin: the human writes and argues over the spec, reviews plans, "Then you leave for 12 hours, and check to see if the tests pass." He places himself there ("I'm here.") and says of level 3 "almost everyone tops out here". He also says "level 2, and every level after it, feels like you are done. But you are not done." - Willison's lower-cost alternative to scenario swarms (10 Feb 2026): "Part of their solution involves expensive swarms of QA agents running through "scenarios" to exercise their software. It's fascinating, but I don't want to spend thousands of dollars on QA robots if I can avoid it! I need tools that allow agents to clearly demonstrate their work to me, while minimizing the opportunities for them to cheat about what they've done." His Showboat tool has an agent build a Markdown demo document from commands whose real output is captured. He reports gaming there too: "I've also seen agents cheat! Since the demo file is Markdown the agent will sometimes edit that file directly rather than using Showboat, which could result in command outputs that don't reflect what actually happened." **Stanford CodeX: points the earlier notes lacked**

- Same-model blind spots: "When the builder and the inspector share the same blind spots, no amount of test variety fully eliminates the risk that both miss the same thing." and "When a human writes a test, the human brings different assumptions, different mistakes, and different oversights than the person who wrote the code. That mismatch is what makes testing useful. When the same AI model writes the code and evaluates it, that mismatch shrinks." — [Stanford Law CodeX, 8 Feb 2026](https://law.stanford.edu/2026/02/08/built-by-agents-tested-by-agents-trusted-by-whom/)
- Goodhart framing: "Tell an agent to maximize a test score and it will maximize the test score, whether or not the underlying software actually works." "The "return true" episode was a crude version. Subtler versions will be harder to catch." — [Stanford Law CodeX](https://law.stanford.edu/2026/02/08/built-by-agents-tested-by-agents-trusted-by-whom/)
- No standard for the evidence: "No industry standard defines what a sufficient satisfaction score looks like. No audit methodology covers agent-built software tested against replicas. No procurement checklist asks whether the vendor's coding agents share blind spots with the vendor's testing agents." The line "Satisfaction scores exceeded our threshold" appears only as the author's imagined vendor answer, not as a StrongDM statement. — [Stanford Law CodeX](https://law.stanford.edu/2026/02/08/built-by-agents-tested-by-agents-trusted-by-whom/)
- Closing risk statement: "The risk is that when it fails, nobody will know why. Nobody will know how to fix it. And the institutional knowledge required to understand the failure will have atrophied, because the humans stopped reading code years ago." — [Stanford Law CodeX](https://law.stanford.edu/2026/02/08/built-by-agents-tested-by-agents-trusted-by-whom/)
- The author suggests warranties "tied not to human inspection but to satisfaction scores, scenario coverage, or Digital Twin fidelity", and notes "Nobody has done this yet". This is a proposal, not a report of practice. — [Stanford Law CodeX](https://law.stanford.edu/2026/02/08/built-by-agents-tested-by-agents-trusted-by-whom/)

### Inferences
- StrongDM's twin-fidelity method is differential testing against the real service (run the same calls against twin and live dependency "until we stop finding behavioral differences"), plus, per Jay Taylor via Willison, using the vendor's official SDK client libraries as the compatibility target. That answers part of the earlier gap on fidelity at build time; nothing addresses drift after the real service changes.
- StrongDM's "A sample of the output, fed back into the inputs" combined with "The loop runs until the holdout scenarios pass" implies some signal from validation reaches the building loop. The pages do not say what the coding agent is shown from a failed holdout scenario, so the earlier concern about a holdout eroding through feedback stays open, and the pages give some reason to think the concern is real.
- The two flagship accounts differ sharply on check independence. StrongDM's stated design is behavior-only validation with scenarios kept (often) outside the builder's view. OpenAI's design has the same agent family write product code, tests, evaluation harnesses and review comments, with independence coming from deterministic lints, structural tests, a running app with telemetry, and multiple agent reviewers. OpenAI's page describes no hidden or builder-inaccessible check.
- OpenAI's account is not a no-human pipeline: humans "validate outcomes", set acceptance criteria, may review PRs, and are the escalation point "when judgment is required"; the product also has real users reporting bugs. StrongDM's rule is stricter on code (no human writes or reviews it) but both leave humans defining intent and judging results.
- OpenAI's "minimal blocking merge gates" and retrying flaky tests is the opposite of a strict gate; it relies on cheap correction after merge. A team without OpenAI's throughput and cleanup agents should not copy that part, as the post itself says ("This would be irresponsible in a low-throughput environment").
- Before-and-after video recordings (OpenAI) and executed-command demo documents (Willison's Showboat) are two concrete forms of evidence for a human who does not read the diff, in addition to the scores and evaluator reports the earlier notes listed.

### Gaps
- StrongDM publishes no satisfaction threshold, judge model for satisfaction scoring, scenario count, satisfaction score, defect or incident rate, or description of who authors scenarios (only the caption "Synthetic scenario curation and shaping interface"). Where holdout scenarios are stored and how access is controlled is not stated.
- What a StrongDM human looks at before release, and whether a human approves production deploys, is not on any page read.
- OpenAI gives no defect, rollback or incident numbers, no count or description of the "specific agent reviews", and no statement on whether a human approves production deploys. It does not say how often humans actually review PRs.
- How OpenAI prevents Codex from weakening its own tests or lints is not addressed on the page.
- Shapiro gives no account of how level 5 teams verify output; his post is a taxonomy of human roles.
- The strongdm.com (now redirecting to delinea.com) blog version of the Software Factory post was not readable, so any detail unique to it is missing.
