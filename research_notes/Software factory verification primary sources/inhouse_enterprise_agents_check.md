## Which earlier claims hold up exactly, which need correcting, which cannot be confirmed?

### Takeaway
Almost every numeric and verification-layer claim in the earlier notes holds up against the primary page, usually word for word. Four things need correcting: the Google "39 migrations / 74.45%" figures come from a different paper than the one cited; Spotify's removal of the LLM judge is confirmed in the Spotify speakers' own words (it was listed as unconfirmed); Uber's "11%" is PRs opened by agents in general, not by Minion specifically, and the Minion self-validation details sit behind a paywall; and several items tagged secondhand (Stripe's lint timings, Ramp's PR screenshots, Meta's "41 candidates" and "RubFake") are in fact on the primary pages.

**Spotify — Honk** (Part 1 dated Nov 6 2025, Part 3 dated Dec 9 2025, both by Max Charas and Marc Bruggmann; Part 4 dated Apr 22 2026)
- CONFIRMED, verifiers, and every detail in the [recall, not re-verified] item: "An individual verifier is not exposed directly to the agent; instead, it activates automatically depending on the software component contents. For example, a Maven verifier activates if it finds a pom.xml file in the root of the codebase." "The agent is unaware of the underlying Maven verifier." - CONFIRMED, the judge and its numbers: "The judge is simple. It uses the diff of the proposed change and the original prompt, and sends them to an LLM for evaluation. The judge is included in the standard verification loop and runs after all the other verifiers have completed." "We have yet to invest in evals for our judge. However, we know from internal metrics that out of thousands of agent sessions, the judge vetoes about a quarter of them. When that happens, the agent is able to course correct half the time. From empirical observations, we have seen that the most common trigger is the agent going outside the instructions outlined in the prompt." — [Honk Part 3](https://engineering.atspotify.com/2025/12/feedback-loops-background-coding-agents-part-3)
- CONFIRMED, 1,500+ PRs: "To date, our agents have generated more than 1,500 pull requests that teams across Spotify have merged into our production codebase." — [Spotify, Honk Part 1](https://engineering.atspotify.com/2025/11/spotifys-background-coding-agent-part-1)
- CORRECTED from "no Spotify primary source confirming removal" to CONFIRMED: the judge was removed. In the transcript of the QCon London 2026 talk by Spotify's Jo Kelly-Fenton and Aleksandar Mitic: "Then we started to see some issues with our judge... the judge is like, the post method wasn't migrated to send message, and so it blocks the migration from continuing, but the LLM did the right thing. Our judge was simple, but in a bad way. As we found the models getting better and better, we saw less and less need for an LLM-as-a-judge. If you just put verification steps within your prompt, the LLMs are getting really good at doing that themselves. We ended up actually removing the LLM-as-a-judge eventually, and we haven't really looked back. It's not that they're inherently bad. There's probably a place for them. We haven't really found the need for them. The two accounts are the same account, not a conflict. Spotify's own blog posts (Parts 3 and 4) do not mention the removal.

### Inferences
- The earlier notes' table of layers survives the check with three edits: Spotify's LLM judge row should read "added 2025, removed by early 2026 (speakers' own account)"; Uber's "agent drives the running app / staging deploys" row has no readable source and should be marked unverified; Google's row should cite arXiv 2504.09691 for the 74%/39 figures.
- Ramp's share figures are not one series: "written by Inspect" (Jan 2026, ~30%) and "raised by Inspect" / "come from Inspect sessions" (Aug 2026, 75%) may count different things. Likewise Uber's 11% counts PRs "opened by agents", and Spotify's "around half of Spotify’s pull requests have been automated" refers to the pre-LLM Fleet Management system, not Honk.
- Meta's 73% acceptance figure is attached to two different populations in Meta's own material (Messenger and WhatsApp test-a-thons in the paper; a wider Oct–Dec 2024 trial in the blog). A report should cite it as "73% of ACH-generated tests accepted by Meta engineers" and name which source it follows.
- "Played a key role in catching 74" and "lowering the overall occurrence of privacy SEVs by 20%" are Meta's wording; the post gives no method for attributing the 20% drop to JiTTests, so it is a self-reported association, not a measured causal effect.

## Does any primary page describe end-to-end verification against a running system, protected tests, held-out checks, or post-deploy gates for agent changes that the earlier notes missed?

### Takeaway
Yes for running-system evidence and for CI-before-PR, no for protected tests or held-out checks. The missed items are: Ramp's integration factory attaching a recorded run against the real provider to each PR; Spotify moving to a separate verification service that runs the real CI build before a PR is created, plus auto-merge of some fleet changes and an admitted production failure that passed checks; Meta's JiTTests running overnight on risk-scored diffs after submission; Google's ordered validator chain that discards failing files; and Atlassian's stated principle that model output must not be a release gate. No page read describes a rule that agents may not edit tests, a protected test path, or a held-out acceptance suite; Google and Spotify both describe agents changing or disabling tests.

### Cited Findings

**Verification against a running or real system**
- Ramp, integration factory (builders.ramp.com, "Integrations That Write Themselves", dated 2026-08-14; found in the same site bundle): a custom Inspect agent "researches the APIs, builds the connector, tests and auto-fixes with test credentials, and opens a pull request." The PR carries evidence: "The factory hands over its evidence in the PR: every endpoint it touched, the request it made, the response the real provider returned, and product level screen recordings of the new connectors' tests in action. You don't have to trust the code. You have to trust the artifacts." "Autonomy is bounded by verification, not by code generation... What let us merge factory code was the evidence attached to it, a recorded run against the real provider for every endpoint. An agent can only be trusted as far as its work can be checked cheaply." Scope limit stated by Ramp: "The other half of why it was safe to merge as-is: it touched no shared code: a new provider in its own module within an isolated connectors service rather than the monolith". Tests run "against test credentials and synthetic values, not customer data." This is the first primary mention of screen recordings as PR evidence, which the earlier notes said was absent. — [Ramp Builders site bundle](https://builders.ramp.com/assets/index-DaCNdoGx.js) (article text; the post's own URL slug was not confirmed)
- Ramp, runtime checks after shipping (same post, customer-built integrations): the agent "tests the Checkr integration before it's usable at all: auto-fixing 4xx/5xx errors and checking that a successful response actually means what it should (because a provider accepting a call isn't the same as the call being right). This is the most important metric to track". No post-deploy watch is described. — [Ramp Builders](https://builders.ramp.com/post/why-we-built-our-background-agent)
- Ramp, related tools named in the free part of the newsletter: "ReviewBuddy: Ramp’s own code review system, customizable per team" and "Testo: a frontend QA tool and browser-based agent that clicks around like a user would, and creates Playwright tests." — [Pragmatic Engineer, Aug 25 2026](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/why-ramp-built-inspect)
- Stripe: verification stays at lint plus CI tests. The devbox "contains our source code and runs services under development" and can "start a web service", but neither post says a minion exercises a running service or a browser. Devboxes "run in our QA environment, and consequently, minions don’t have access to real user data, Stripe’s production services, or arbitrary network egress." — [Stripe, Minions Part 2](https://stripe.dev/blog/minions-stripes-one-shot-end-to-end-coding-agents-part-2)

**CI as a gate before the PR exists**
- Spotify, verification service (talk transcript): "if we could separate out our verification runtime from our agent runtime, then we could leverage the pre-existing CI system that's already set up... At some point before we create a PR, we want to do a full end-to-end check that the code is actually correct. How we do that is we push a branch to GitHub, and then we kick off a build. We created a service called a verification service, which abstracts several of our CI systems... If it's incorrect, the agent goes again, but if it's correct, it'll create a PR... you know that the PRs are going to have a correct CI build, because you've already validated it before you'd even created the PR." This is the "outer loop" Part 3 listed as future work in Dec 2025. — [InfoQ presentation transcript](https://www.infoq.com/presentations/spotify-ai-codebase-migration-agent/); [Honk Part 3](https://engineering.atspotify.com/2025/12/feedback-loops-background-coding-agents-part-3)
- Spotify, limits of the verifiers: "Our current verifiers only run on Linux x86... our iOS applications require macOS hosts to run verifiers successfully". And where repos have no tests the loop is empty: "the BigQuery Runner and dbt repositories across the company rarely used any build-time unit testing. This meant that one of Honk’s key features, its ability to verify its work and then adjust based on the results, was unavailable to us, and we had to rely on the downstream owning teams to perform their own manual testing before merging the automated PRs." — [Honk Part 3](https://engineering.atspotify.com/2025/12/feedback-loops-background-coding-agents-part-3); [Honk Part 4, Apr 22 2026](https://engineering.atspotify.com/2026/4/background-coding-agents-dataset-migrations-honk-part-4)
- Spotify, throughput: "we had 1,000 merged PRs in 3 months... Today, we do this in 10 days", and review is "becoming the new bottleneck." — [InfoQ presentation transcript](https://www.infoq.com/presentations/spotify-ai-codebase-migration-agent/)

**Test weakening: observed, and what is said to stop it**
- Spotify names it as the reason for the judge: "some agents were a bit too “ambitious”, trying to solve problems that weren’t strictly in their prompt, like refactoring code or disabling flaky tests." — [Honk Part 3](https://engineering.atspotify.com/2025/12/feedback-loops-background-coding-agents-part-3). In the talk: agents downgraded a Java version "because that was just the easiest way to get the build to pass", and "the judge would say removing a test was not part of the original requirements, and then the agent would be forced to then uncomment the test and actually fix the underlying issue". After removing the judge, the stated defence is prompt-level verification steps plus "The code and the tests". — [InfoQ presentation transcript](https://www.infoq.com/presentations/spotify-ai-codebase-migration-agent/); [InfoQ, Mar 18 2026](https://www.infoq.com/news/2026/03/spotify-honk-rewrite)
- Spotify's first failure-mode list is itself a statement of the problem: "The background agent produces a PR that passes CI but is functionally incorrect. This is the most serious error, as it erodes trust in our automation." — [Honk Part 3](https://engineering.atspotify.com/2025/12/feedback-loops-background-coding-agents-part-3)
- Google's toolkit edits tests as part of the change: "The migration toolkit runs autonomously and produces verified changes that only contain code that passes unit tests. Some tests are themselves updated to reflect the new reality." — [Google Research blog](https://research.google/blog/accelerating-code-migrations-with-ai/). The later paper's validator chain, in order of cost, includes: "AST parser: If the changed file cannot be parsed with an AST parser... or the AST of the changed file is identical with the original, the change is invalid." "Punt: We pass the files’ contents before and after the change, and ask the LLM whether the change was necessary. If the LLM punts by responding "No", the change is invalid." "Build: If the changed file causes build breakages during compilation, the change is invalid." "Test: If the changed file causes regression test failures, the change is invalid." A failing file "is discarded without running further validations, and marked to be migrated manually by developers"; then "each change is visually investigated by developers for verification... and finally each code change is sent to the owners of the code for final acceptance and submission". — [arXiv 2504.09691 HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.09691)
- Stripe: neither post states any restriction on a minion editing or deleting tests; the post does say failing tests with autofixes are auto-applied and the rest "we send... back to the minion to try and fix". — [Stripe, Minions Part 1](https://stripe.dev/blog/minions-stripes-one-shot-end-to-end-coding-agents)
- Uber uReview has a second-model grader on its own output: "A secondary prompt evaluates each comment’s quality and assigns a confidence score", with thresholds set "per assistant, per language, and comment category"; best configuration "paired Anthropic® Claude-4-Sonnet as the primary comment generator with OpenAI® o4-mini-high as the review grader"; and a human baseline: "only 51% of human-written comments are considered as bugs by the author and addressed in the same changeset." — [Uber Engineering, uReview](https://www.uber.com/blog/ureview/)

**Independent, author-blind checks on a diff**
- Meta JiTTests are not a pre-merge gate on every diff: "The system runs overnight on spare capacity and targets diffs submitted the previous day that are deemed “risky” using a mechanism similar to Diff Risk Score (DRS) and trained on previous code changes." There are two workflows; the one the earlier notes missed is "An “intent-aware” workflow that infers the intention and risks of a code change" which "uses an LLM to infer the diff’s intention using the code changes, diff title, and summary", builds mutants for each risk, generates tests that kill them, and "Those tests are then run on the actual diff. Any that fail are harvested as weak catches." The judge ensemble reviews "The weak-catch test-case code", "The test-execution outcome (failure code, message, execution trace)", "The code changes between parent and child (the diff)" and "The inferred diff intent." Meta contrasts this with AI review: "It generates executable tests that demonstrate a concrete behavioral difference introduced by the change... it catches classes of bugs that LLM- or agent-based code reviews might not." — [Engineering at Meta, Oct 29 2026](https://engineering.fb.com/2026/10/29/security/catching-bugs-before-they-land-jittestings-human-on-the-loop-approach-at-full-scale/)

**Post-merge and post-deploy**
- Atlassian, beyond the closer: "Keep verification deterministic and human-controlled: The agent can prepare a change, but the existing build, tests, branch protections, and required reviews still decide whether it is safe to merge. Model output should not become a release gate by itself." "If the checks fail or the fix falls outside the encoded patterns, the run stops and reports why." "When the build fails, the dependency pattern is unfamiliar, or deployment evidence is missing, stop with a clear handoff." Each job "has a narrow purpose and its own permissions"; the agent runs with "only the Bitbucket permissions required for that step." Task selection required that "The repository’s build and test suite could verify the change." — [Inside Atlassian, Aug 30 2026](https://www.atlassian.com/blog/bitbucket/vulnerability-fixes-with-agentic-pipelines)
- Spotify auto-merges some automated fleet changes and has had one escape: "Our custom Fleet Management framework has for years made large scale changes across our fleet every day, with the vast majority merged automatically after passing safety checks. For over a year now, we have expanded this to support more complex agentic-driven changes... This year, an automated dependency upgrade passed our checks, but still failed in production, impacting end users. We are responding by strengthening safeguards, expanding rollback capacity, and scheduling automated changes during owning teams’ working hours." The post does not say that upgrade was agent-authored. — [Spotify Engineering, Sep 16 2026](https://engineering.atspotify.com/2026/9/ai-changed-how-spotify-builds-what-we-learned-and-fixed-about-quality-at-higher-velocity). In the talk, auto-merge predates LLMs ("where we can fully reason about the change, or there's some way to test it, we can even auto-merge") and which agent PRs to auto-merge is open: "The question is which? This is a hard question, and it's not fully answered... Maybe documentation changes can be auto-merged. Maybe internal systems that, if they go down, we just revert". — [InfoQ presentation transcript](https://www.infoq.com/presentations/spotify-ai-codebase-migration-agent/)
- Spotify publishes the nearest thing to an incident comparison for AI-authored code: in monthly incident retrospectives it asks "Did AI-authored code directly contribute to the incident? And did the increased volume of change put additional pressure on review, testing, rollout, or observability?" Result: "Across the incidents reviewed so far, we did not identify AI-authored code as a material direct contributor. We did, however, observe the second risk: the volume of change increased faster than some of our verification controls could adapt." Also: "Total merged changes more than doubled year over year in August, from roughly 8,100 to 17,000"; "we see no corresponding rise in rework rate"; "code complexity and PR size are both creeping up"; "AI increased the capacity to produce change. The next constraint became our ability to verify it." Self-reported, no counts of incidents given. — [Spotify Engineering, Sep 16 2026](https://engineering.atspotify.com/2026/9/ai-changed-how-spotify-builds-what-we-learned-and-fixed-about-quality-at-higher-velocity)

**Human gate details the earlier notes missed**
- Stripe has two human steps, not one: "If the code looks good, the engineer opens the PR and requests a review from another Stripe engineer. If not, they can give the minion further instructions". The requesting engineer checks first; a second engineer reviews. — [Stripe, Minions Part 1](https://stripe.dev/blog/minions-stripes-one-shot-end-to-end-coding-agents)
- Stripe frames deterministic steps as a reliability and cost measure: "writing code to deterministically accomplish small decisions we can anticipate—such as “always lint changes at the end of a run”—saves tokens (and CI costs) at scale and gives the agent a little less opportunity to get things wrong." — [Stripe, Minions Part 2](https://stripe.dev/blog/minions-stripes-one-shot-end-to-end-coding-agents-part-2)
- Spotify limits the agent's reach as a predictability measure: "The agent itself has very limited access. It can see the relevant codebase, use tools to edit files, and execute verifiers as tools." "Pushing code, interacting with users on Slack, and even the authoring of prompts are all managed by surrounding infrastructure." — [Honk Part 3](https://engineering.atspotify.com/2025/12/feedback-loops-background-coding-agents-part-3)

### Inferences
- The earlier conclusion "no company publishes a protected-tests or held-out-checks rule" stands after reading the primaries. The closest things on record are structural: Meta's catching tests are generated by a separate system and judged against the parent revision; Spotify's (now removed) judge compared diff to prompt; Google discards a file whose change fails build or regression tests but also lets the tool update tests; Atlassian keeps "branch protections, and required reviews" as the deciding gate.
- Spotify's history is the one documented case of adding and then removing an LLM veto: about a quarter of sessions vetoed, half recovered, then removed because it blocked correct changes on repos that did not match the judge's expectations. The replacement was not "no check" but a stronger deterministic one (the real CI build before PR creation) plus in-prompt verification steps. That supports reading the removal as "deterministic CI displaced the LLM judge", which is my inference; the speakers attribute it to better models.
- Two primaries now describe PR evidence richer than screenshots: Ramp's factory (request/response logs from the real provider and screen recordings) and Atlassian (deployed-commit evidence before a ticket moves). Both are narrow, isolated task classes, and Ramp says explicitly that isolation from shared code was half of why merging as-is was safe.
- The "canary / auto-rollback tied to agent changes: none found" row still holds. Spotify's Sept 2026 post is the nearest: rollback capacity and working-hours scheduling for automated changes generally, after an automated change passed checks and failed in production.
- Meta's JiTTests should not be described as a merge gate: they run the night after submission on risk-scored diffs and reach the author only when ranked highly, so they are an asynchronous, selective signal.

### Gaps
- No primary read here says whether Stripe, Ramp or Uber restrict agent edits to existing tests, or how often agents weaken tests.
- Stripe publishes no figure for how often a minion exhausts the two-round cap, and no share of total PRs.
- The Ramp factory post's canonical URL was not fetched (its text came from the site bundle), so cite it by title and date, "Integrations That Write Themselves", Ramp Builders, 2026-08-14, and confirm the link before publication.
- Whether Spotify's failed "automated dependency upgrade" was produced by Honk or by a pre-LLM Fleet Management script is not stated.
